Four of the world’s greatest physicists discuss, in non­
technical language, the ways we see and think about the 
world in the age of nuclear physics.
Werner Heisenberg (1901- 
) won the Nobel Prize in
1932 for his development of quantum mechanics. He is 
best known for his formulation of the Uncertainty Prin­
ciple, which states the impossibility of simultaneous, ac­
curate determination of both the position and the velocity 
of a particle. During World War II, he was director of the 
Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Physics in Berlin. A t present, 
he is director of the M ax Planck Institute for Physics and 
Astrophysics in Munich.
M ax Born (1882- 
) was co-winner of the 1954
Nobel Prize for his statistical studies on wave functions. 
He is also well-known as a writer on science for the lay­
man. Bom  in Breslau, he taught physics at Göttingen, 
Berlin, and Frankfurt-am-Main until he fled Germany in 
1933. He taught at Cambridge and Edinburgh, became a 
British citizen in 1939 and presently resides in Bad 
Pyrmont, Germany.
Erwin Schrödinger (1887-1961) was co-winner of the 
Nobel Prize in 1933 for his work on wave mechanics and 
its application to atomic structure. He was bom and edu­
cated in Vienna and succeeded M ax Planck as professor 
of physics at the University of Berlin in 1927. He taught 
in Dublin in 1940 and returned to Vienna in 1956.
Pierre Auger (1899- 
) has headed the French Com­
mittee on Space Research since 1960 and is the general 
secretary of the European Center for Space Research. 
From 1948 to 1958 he was director of the Natural Sci­
ences Department of U N E S C O . He has written on X-rays, 
neutrons, and cosmic rays and on the philosophy of 
science.
